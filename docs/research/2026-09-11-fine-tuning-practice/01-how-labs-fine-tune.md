# How labs actually fine-tune small models — and what the lever is

Context: our project fine-tuned Qwen3.5-4B / Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 with LoRA (rank 16 SFT / 8 DPO, all-linear, last-16-of-32 layers, seq 1024-2048, LR 1e-4 SFT / 5e-6 DPO, 2 epochs, batch 1×8, bf16, completion-only loss) across four iterations (SFT on 388 execution-verified suites, DPO on 497 pairs, SFT on 358 execution-trace targets, DPO on 645 pairs) with all data self-generated (no external teacher) — all null/marginal (+0.024 [-0.013,+0.064], n=315).

## 1. Data scale and provenance

Every lab source we found runs orders of magnitude more data than our 388-645 examples, and the responses in that data are almost never self-generated alone.

- **Qwen2.5** (base for the coder/chat lines): "over 1 million samples are employed for supervised fine-tuning" in general post-training [1].
- **Qwen2.5-Coder**: a dedicated code-SFT stage uses "200K synthetic instruction–completion pairs, generated by few-shot prompting and verified against curated standards" [2] — note "verified," i.e., filtered by an external check, not accepted wholesale.
- **Tülu 3** (Allen AI): 939,344 SFT prompts total (57% public, 43% synthetic), with **all new synthetic responses generated by GPT-4o or Claude 3.5 Sonnet** (Claude specifically for coding) [3]. Preference data converts roughly 2-300K of those prompts into pairs, and — importantly — **on-policy completions from the Tülu3-SFT model itself are added to the preference set and reported as beneficial** [3]. That's the closest primary-source evidence we found that self-generated data helps, but only as a *supplement* inside a pipeline still anchored by GPT-4o/Claude-judged preferences and an LLM-as-judge (GPT-4o) rating responses 1-5 — not as a stand-alone self-improvement loop with a weak base model.
- **Llama 3** (Meta): 6 rounds of rejection-sampling → SFT → DPO. Each round samples K=10-30 outputs per prompt from the *current* chat policy and a trained reward model picks the best [4]. This is self-generated in the sense that completions come from Llama 3 itself, but it is scaffolded by (a) a separately-trained reward model, (b) human-annotated preference data feeding that reward model, and (c) millions of examples (2.7M+ synthetic examples for code SFT alone) [4]. Self-generation only pays off here because there's a reward model trained on human judgments doing the selection — not because raw self-sampling improves quality on its own.
- **Qwen3** RL stage: only 3,995 curated query-verifier pairs, but they are heavily filtered (excluded from cold-start, "as challenging as possible," broad sub-domain coverage) and trained with large batch sizes, high rollout counts per query, and off-policy training [5]. Small *curated* verifier sets can work, but curation quality and rollout volume substitute for raw prompt count — neither of which we had (no filtering beyond "did our own harness pass," no rollout-based exploration).
- **NVIDIA Nemotron Nano 2**: SFT stage alone runs ~90B tokens (~11.5M samples across domains, e.g. 1.1M coding, 1.5M math) [9]; GRPO stage samples 16,000 prompts from LMSYS Chat with IFEval augmentation [9].

**Answer to "does self-generated data without a stronger teacher ever produce gains, and at what scale":** the only unambiguous primary-source case is Llama 3's rejection-sampling loop, and it requires a reward model trained on human preference labels plus 6 rounds and millions of examples — not raw self-sampling at hundreds of examples. Tülu 3's on-policy addition is a minority ingredient (a fraction of a 200-300K pair set) layered on top of GPT-4o/Claude-generated and GPT-4o-judged data, not a standalone driver. We found no source claiming a self-only loop works at the 300-700 example scale we used.

## 2. LoRA/QLoRA hyperparameters

**Unsloth's documented defaults** [6][8]: rank 16 or 32 as the default sweep (8/16/32/64/128 available); **alpha = rank, or "r×2" as a common heuristic**, with the rule that alpha/rank should stay ≥1; target **all linear layers** (q/k/v/o + gate/up/down) because "the VRAM cost of all-linear vs. just q/v is small and the quality gain is consistent"; LR 2e-4 for standard SFT LoRA vs. **5e-6 for RL methods (DPO, GRPO)** — our DPO LR of 5e-6 matches this exactly, which is a point in favor of that part of our recipe; 1-3 epochs, with an explicit rule for small datasets: **"on fewer than 500 examples, use 1-2 epochs maximum"** [8] — our 550 training functions sit right at that boundary and 2 epochs is within range. Effective batch size target 4-16 (we used 8, in range). Weight decay 0.01-0.1, warmup 5-10% of steps, LoRA dropout 0 by default. Loss masking: **"train on completions only to increase accuracy by approximately 1%"** [6] — presented as close to a default, matching what we did.

The RLVR-for-code paper on Qwen3-0.6B/Llama3.2-1B used LoRA rank 32, alpha 64, all attention + MLP projections, LR 1e-5 [7] — a higher rank than our SFT rank 16 and roughly double our DPO rank 8.

**Gap:** our DPO LoRA rank of 8 is below every documented default (16-32). Unsloth explicitly frames rank 8 as an "underfitting" risk to fix by *raising* rank, not lowering it [6]. If alpha in our DPO stage was not set to 8 or 16 (2×rank), that combination is inside the documented failure zone.

## 3. RL with verifiable rewards (GRPO/RLVR) on small models for code

This is the strongest single data point for a *feasible, positive, reproducible* result at our scale. The RLVR-for-code paper [7] trained Qwen3-0.6B and Llama3.2-1B with LoRA GRPO/GSPO on **374 MBPP tasks**, 8 rollouts per prompt, batch size 1, 5 epochs, on a single RTX 3090 (24GB) — smaller compute than our M4 Pro 48GB. Reported gains: **+14.4pp pass@1** for Qwen3-0.6B with a combined (unit-test + static-analysis) reward, +13.9pp with unit-tests alone; a static-analysis-only reward *degraded* results by 2-4pp, and Llama3.2-1B's gains were smaller (+3-4pp) [7]. Two things stand out: (a) the reward was still self-referential (their own test harness), similar to our mutation-kill signal, and it still worked — so self-verification-based reward isn't inherently doomed; (b) the difference from our failed DPO iterations is the training signal is **online rollout + policy gradient (GRPO/GSPO)**, not offline pairwise preference from a fixed, already-collected dataset.

On Mac feasibility: `mlx-lm-lora` explicitly supports GRPO (branded "Dr. GRPO") alongside SFT/DPO/ORPO/CPO/PPO, runnable with LoRA adapters on Apple Silicon [10]. Practical throughput reporting is thin, but general MLX guidance notes memory-bandwidth (not compute) is the M-series ceiling relative to datacenter GPUs [10] — plausible but unverified at our specific scale. Qwen3's own RL stage explicitly credits **large batch size and high rollout count per query** as beneficial [5], which is the opposite of our DPO setup (offline pairs, no rollout diversity per prompt).

**We never ran GRPO/RLVR.** Given it's the only method in this survey with a *documented, replicated, small-model, code-verifiable-reward, single-GPU, positive result* at a scale close to ours, this is the highest-priority untried lever.

## 4. What labs say does NOT work / common mistakes

- **Too few epochs on small data, or too many** — Unsloth: on <500 examples, cap at 1-2 epochs; general cap 1-3, since "more than 3 on a small dataset overfits" [8]. Training loss approaching 0 is flagged as the overfitting tripwire, with an explicit fix list: fewer epochs, higher weight decay, or scaling LoRA alpha down [6].
- **LoRA rank too low relative to task difficulty** — Unsloth's underfitting checklist starts with "increase LoRA rank and alpha" [6], implying rank 8 is a common under-capacity mistake, not a tuned choice.
- **Narrow-domain fine-tuning causing catastrophic forgetting** — general guidance: mitigate with lower LR, fewer epochs, and mixing 10-20% general-instruction data back in [8]; we trained purely on our narrow task with no general-data admixture.
- **Wrong reward shape in RLVR** — the code-RLVR paper found a static-analysis-only reward caused *degenerate, shorter, less-correct* outputs; only a combined correctness+quality reward avoided this [7]. Relevant warning for us since our reward (mutation kills) is also a single narrow signal.
- **DPO without SFT warm start** — no source recommends this; Tülu 3, Llama 3, and Unsloth all run SFT before DPO/RLVR [3][4][6]. We did follow this ordering correctly each iteration.
- **Relying on a fixed offline preference set instead of on-policy/rollout diversity** — Tülu 3 explicitly adds on-policy completions from its own SFT model to the preference mix and calls it beneficial [3]; Qwen3's RL benefits are tied to high rollout counts per query [5]. Our DPO pairs were static (497, then 645), generated once, not resampled online against an evolving policy.

## 5. What we did not follow, ranked by evidence strength for a 4B model on a verifiable task at ~500 functions

1. **Data scale (2-3 orders of magnitude short).** Every source runs 100K-1M+ SFT examples or hundreds of thousands of preference pairs [1][2][3][4][9]; we used 358-645. This is the most consistently cited lever across every lab and the most obviously violated.
2. **No online RL with verifiable rewards (GRPO/RLVR).** The one primary-source result matching our exact problem shape (small model, code, self-referential test-based reward, consumer-scale hardware) shows double-digit pass@1 gains with GRPO/GSPO [7], and it's directly runnable via `mlx-lm-lora` on our hardware [10]. We ran only offline DPO, never rollout-based RL.
3. **Self-generated data without a stronger teacher, at small scale.** No source shows this working stand-alone; Llama 3's version needs a human-preference-trained reward model and millions of examples [4], Tülu 3's version is a minority supplement to teacher-generated data [3]. Using 100% self-generated data with no teacher, no reward model, and no scale is the most theoretically unsupported choice in our recipe.
4. **DPO LoRA rank (8) below documented default band (16-32)** [6][7] — plausibly compounds with low data volume rather than being independently decisive.
5. **No general-data admixture / regularization against narrow-task drift** — Llama 3's DPO adds an explicit NLL regularization term on the chosen response [4]; we had no equivalent anti-drift term, though this is a second-order effect next to (1)-(3).
6. **Single round, no expert-iteration structure.** Llama 3 runs 6 rounds of resample→SFT→DPO [4]; we ran what amounts to one round per method per iteration, with no reward-model-gated resampling loop.

Items 6 and 5 are ranked below the top three because the magnitude of the data-scale gap and the absence of any tried-and-documented small-model RL method plausibly swamp incremental structural fixes — no source suggests round-count or regularization terms rescue a 500-example, no-teacher, offline-DPO setup.
